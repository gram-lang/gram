In [Your First Recipe](/docs/tutorials/first-recipe), the **Lemon Meringue Tart** was written from start to finish in a single `.gram` file, from the sweet pastry dough to the curd. But in cooking as in software, foundational components — a pastry crust, a stock, or a ganache — are reusable building blocks you turn to across dozens of dishes.

Extracting these core components into their own dedicated files eliminates repetitive copy-pasting: whenever you refine your dough (a dialed-in resting time, a tweaked flour ratio), every tart recipe in your collection immediately benefits from the improvement.

This tutorial guides you through extracting that dough into a reusable component and importing it with `@use` — Gram's module system.

<Steps>

1.  **Extract the base into its own file**

    Start from the dough section written in the first tutorial:

    ```gram title="Original section"
    ## Sweet Pastry Dough ->&pastry dough{}

    [Process] In a #food processor{}, the @flour{180g}, @icing sugar{55g}, and @salt{1/4 tsp}.

    [Crumble] Add the @butter{115g}(cold, cut into small cubes) and mix for ~{1-2min} until sandy.

    [Combine] Add the @egg{1}, @?vanilla extract{1/2 tsp} and mix until the dough comes together.

    [Rest] Wrap in plastic and let it rest in the fridge for ~_{1h}.
    ```

    Move this preparation into its own file at `bases/sweet-pastry-dough.gram`. Two adjustments accompany this move:

    *   **Variable naming is now defined by the import**: the `->&pastry dough{}` declaration on the section header is no longer mandatory for a single-purpose module. When importing the file, you choose its local binding name directly with `as &dough` (`@use "..." as &dough`). For complex modules that produce multiple distinct components, declaring `->&` on each section remains the standard way to export each part by name (see [What a module exports](/docs/reference/syntax/modules#what-a-module-exports)).
    *   **Gram measures the module's yield automatically**: whenever an importing recipe asks for a specific amount (such as `&dough{250g}`), Gram sums this file's own ingredient masses to work out how much it actually produces, then scales every quantity in it by that ratio — nothing to declare or keep in sync as the recipe evolves.

    ```gram title="bases/sweet-pastry-dough.gram"
    ---
    title: 'Sweet Pastry Dough'
    ---

    ## Sweet Pastry Dough

    [Process] In a #food processor{}, the @flour{180g}, @icing sugar{55g}, and @salt{1/4 tsp}.

    [Crumble] Add the @butter{115g}(cold, cut into small cubes) and mix for ~{1-2min} until sandy.

    [Combine] Add the @egg{1}, @?vanilla extract{1/2 tsp} and mix until the dough comes together.

    [Rest] Wrap in plastic and let it rest in the fridge for ~_{1h}.
    ```

    :::tip[How the yield is measured]
    Gram adds up every ingredient's mass in the file — 180g flour, 55g sugar, 115g butter, and so on — to work out this module's own total yield, then scales every quantity in it by whatever ratio the importer's request implies. If any of that sum leans on an estimated density or default unit weight rather than an explicit mass, Gram flags the resulting scale factor as an estimate rather than treating it as exact (see [Scaling](/docs/reference/syntax/modules#scaling)).
    :::

2.  **Import the base into your recipe**

    Back in your tart recipe, replace the dough section with an `@use` directive placed right after the frontmatter, before the first step:

    ```gram title="lemon-tart.gram"
    ---
    title: Lemon Meringue Tart
    portions: 8
    ---

    @use "./bases/sweet-pastry-dough.gram" as &dough

    ## Lemon Curd ->&curd

    [Whisk] In a #saucepan{}, the @lemon zest{1 tbsp}<@lemon, @lemon juice{120g}<@lemon{2}, @sugar{125% @&lemon juice}, and @eggs{3}.

    [Cook] Over ^{medium heat} for ~{8min} until thickened.

    ## Baking the Tart Shell ->&baked shell{}

    [Preheat] The #oven to ^{350F}.

    [Roll out] The &dough{450g} for ~{5min} and place it in a #tart ring{}.

    [Bake] For ~_{20min} until golden. Let cool.

    ## Assembly

    [Pour] The &curd into the &baked shell{}. Chill in the fridge for ~_{2h}.
    ```

    Notice what changed in the recipe body:

    *   `&pastry dough{}` (an internal variable reference) became `&dough{450g}` (an imported binding, requested with an explicit quantity).
    *   Everything downstream — the Gantt timeline, the shopping list, and nutritional values — works seamlessly. The dough's `~_{1h}` rest still interleaves with making the lemon curd, because `@use` inlines the imported steps into the global schedule.

    Requesting `&dough{450g}` against a module yielding `450g` produces a scale factor of 1 (a full batch). If you ask for less (e.g. `&dough{250g}` for a smaller tart), Gram automatically scales all ingredients in `sweet-pastry-dough.gram` down to match.

3.  **Reuse the base across multiple recipes**

    Here is the real payoff. A second recipe — for example, a batch of jam tartlets — can import the exact same base file:

    ```gram title="jam-tartlets.gram"
    ---
    title: Jam Tartlets
    ---

    @use "./bases/sweet-pastry-dough.gram" as &dough

    ## Tartlets

    [Divide] The &dough{450g} into 12 small tart tins.

    [Bake] At ^{180C} for ~_{15min} until golden.

    [Fill] Each shell with a spoonful of @jam{15g}.
    ```

    Your project directory now looks like this:

    <FileTree>
    - bases/
      - sweet-pastry-dough.gram
    - jam-tartlets.gram
    - lemon-tart.gram
    </FileTree>

    Whenever you refine `bases/sweet-pastry-dough.gram` (adjusting oven temperature, tweaking resting time, or tuning flour ratios), both recipes instantly benefit from the update during compilation. No manual copy-pasting required.

</Steps>

## Encapsulation & Math Scoping

A module's relative quantities and ingredient dependencies (`@sugar{125% @&lemon juice}` in the curd) resolve only against *the sections within that specific module*. A host recipe that uses the same ingredient names elsewhere never collides with a base's internal math, and vice versa. 

This strict section-scoping guarantees that splitting a recipe into files never introduces unexpected side effects. See [Encapsulation](/docs/reference/syntax/modules#encapsulation) for complete details.
